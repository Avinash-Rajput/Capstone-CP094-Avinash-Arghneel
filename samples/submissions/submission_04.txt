Last semester I ran a small experiment on myself. For thirty days I deleted every social app from my phone and kept a diary of how my schoolwork changed. I expected nothing dramatic, but the results surprised me.

During the first week I felt restless and strangely lonely. I kept reaching for my pocket without thinking, and I worried that my friends were planning things without me. By the second week, however, my evenings felt longer. I finished a novel for my literature class that I had been avoiding for months, and I started arriving at morning tutorials with my reading actually done.

The biggest change showed up in my marks. My statistics quiz scores rose from the low sixties to the high seventies, mostly because I was practising problems instead of half watching clips. My essays also improved, since I had time to write a proper draft and then revise it.

The experiment was not perfect. I missed a few invitations, and one group assignment became harder because my teammates organised everything through a chat I had left. That taught me that these apps are not purely harmful. They are woven into how young people communicate, and quitting completely can isolate a student.

Today I use a middle path. The apps stay on my laptop only, and I check them twice a day at fixed times. My conclusion is simple. Social media itself does not ruin academic performance, but unlimited and unplanned use quietly steals the hours that learning requires.
