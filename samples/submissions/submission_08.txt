My argument is narrow but important. Social media is changing the way students read and write, and those two skills sit underneath almost every subject they study.

Posts on most apps are short, informal and quickly forgotten. Young people spend hours reading captions of a dozen words and replying with slang, emojis and abbreviations. Over time, this habit trains the brain to expect instant meaning. When the same reader opens a history textbook with long paragraphs and careful arguments, the text feels slow and exhausting. Teachers in my school say that fewer pupils finish the novels set for literature class, and many admit they only read online summaries.

Writing suffers in a similar way. Informal spelling and missing punctuation start appearing in formal essays. Some students struggle to build a paragraph that develops one idea from beginning to end, because they are used to expressing opinions in a single line. Examiners notice these weaknesses, and they cost marks in every subject that requires extended answers, including science and economics.

To be fair, social media does encourage some writing. Students who run blogs or write detailed reviews can actually become more confident authors. The problem is balance. Very few teenagers balance their quick posts with an equal amount of serious reading.

I believe the answer lies in deliberate practice. Schools could protect a daily silent reading period, assign longer writing tasks with careful feedback, and openly discuss the difference between online language and academic language. If students learn to switch between these two styles, their performance across the curriculum will improve.
