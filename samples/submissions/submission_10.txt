Most debates about social media and academic achievement focus on individual willpower. I think this misses the point. Students are teenagers competing against companies that employ thousands of designers, so responsibility must be shared by schools, families and the technology industry.

Schools are the first line of action. Several countries have already banned phones in classrooms, and early reports from those schools describe calmer lessons and more conversation between pupils. However, a ban alone is not enough. Young people also need digital literacy lessons that explain how recommendation algorithms work, why some content is engineered to provoke outrage, and how to judge whether a source is trustworthy.

Families play an equally important role. Parents who scroll through dinner send a clear message about priorities. Simple household agreements, such as charging every device in the kitchen overnight, can model healthy habits without turning parents into police officers.

Finally, governments and companies must accept some duty. Age limits are rarely enforced, and features such as infinite feeds and streak rewards are deliberately designed to keep children online. Regulators could require safer default settings for younger users, just as they require seatbelts in cars.

In conclusion, the impact of social media on academic performance is not simply a story about lazy or distracted students. It is the result of a system that rewards constant engagement. If schools teach critical skills, families set reasonable boundaries and lawmakers demand responsible design, students will have a fair chance to use these tools for learning rather than being used by them.
