Critics love to describe social media as the enemy of education, but this view ignores how much genuine learning now happens online. For my generation, an app can be a lecture hall, a language lab and a careers fair all at once.

Consider educational creators. Thousands of teachers, scientists and university graduates publish free explainer videos on everything from organic chemistry to Shakespeare. A struggling pupil who cannot afford private tuition can watch the same lesson five times until the idea finally clicks. Language learners follow native speakers, copy their pronunciation and practise vocabulary through daily challenges that feel like games.

Social networks also widen ambition. Students in small villages can see engineers, doctors and artists describing their real working lives, which makes those careers feel reachable. Professional networking sites allow older students to share portfolios, find internships and ask experienced people for advice. Motivation rises when a young person can picture where their effort might lead.

There is also a creative benefit. Students who run science pages or post book reviews are practising writing, editing and communicating with a real audience. Many universities now value this kind of initiative in their admission decisions.

Naturally, misuse exists, and nobody should pretend that scrolling through jokes is the same as studying. Yet the solution is guidance rather than fear. When teachers recommend trustworthy channels and parents show interest in what their children watch, social media becomes a powerful ally. In my opinion, its overall effect on academic performance is positive for students who are taught to use it wisely.
