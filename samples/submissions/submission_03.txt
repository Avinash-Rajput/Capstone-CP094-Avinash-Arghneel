When people talk about social media and school, they usually mention distraction, but I believe the bigger problem is emotional. Every day, students scroll past carefully edited photos of classmates who seem happier, richer and more successful than they are. This constant comparison slowly wears down self-esteem, and a student who feels worthless rarely has the energy to study. Psychologists have linked heavy use of photo apps to anxiety and loneliness.

Anxiety affects learning in very practical ways. A worried mind struggles to remember formulas, and exam nerves become much worse when a student already doubts their own ability. Online bullying adds another layer of stress, because hurtful comments can follow a victim home and appear on their screen at any hour. Some victims stop attending classes altogether, which leaves gaps in their knowledge that are hard to fill later. Even students who are never bullied may feel pressure to collect likes, as if popularity were a scoreboard.

On the other hand, social media can also provide emotional support. Students who feel isolated sometimes find online communities where they can share worries about exams and receive encouragement from strangers. Mental health campaigns have also helped some teenagers ask for professional help.

In my view, schools should treat social media as a wellbeing issue rather than only a discipline issue. Counsellors, teachers and parents need to talk openly about comparison, anxiety and cyberbullying, so that students can protect both their confidence and their academic results.
